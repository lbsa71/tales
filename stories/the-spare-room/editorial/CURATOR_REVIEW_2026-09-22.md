## Assessment received

The curator saw a worthwhile play and supported a structured development workshop, while finding insufficient evidence for a full production commitment. The central risk was cumulative exhaustion from precise, self-correcting dialogue rather than difficult vocabulary.

The reviewer understood the central action: Helen wants Ruth to move, Ruth decides she wants to go, and Helen becomes the obstacle. Each generation wants independence while trying to retain the version of a loved person that reassures them. An arrangement created through genuine care can become oppressive without making either person a simple villain. The final hand can make chosen accommodation an act of love.

The strongest evidence was theatrical: the withheld bag and its two handles, the moved chair, Ruth's rejection of Helen's sleeve assistance and acceptance of Kit's, and the final offered hand. Shared pleasure in the harbour, miscalled dance, pigeons and Helen's comparison table was essential because it gave the family something worth losing.

The main concerns were:

1. **Repeated explanation after understanding.** One substantial insight is revisited through distinctions which can be correct without making another dramatic advance.
2. **A common rhetorical habit.** Statement, correction, counter-correction and a clever last line recur so often that three characters can sound like one author. Distressed people rarely sustain this level of verbal finish.
3. **Simultaneous comprehension demands.** Spectators must track disputed family history, changing meanings of ordinary words, unfamiliar access rules and emotional consequences without rereading.
4. **Delayed basic orientation.** The direct residence explanation arrives around 43 percent through the supplied text, and centuries become explicit around 70 percent. Earlier clues help, but spectators may spend too long constructing an unrelated residential-care, dementia or ghost story. These are text-position observations, not measured performance timings.
5. **Reader knowledge exceeding stage evidence.** Directions describing Helen as both elsewhere and present identify an intended effect without giving a local company reliable means of producing it.
6. **Dependence on exceptional interpretation.** Strong performers might solve the problems, but that possibility does not make the script sufficiently robust. Confusion or boredom must not be attributed to audience inadequacy.

The proposed next evidence was a small blocked workshop using the actual ordinary objects, with no explanatory directions read aloud and no briefing that reveals the premise. Listeners should report where Ruth lives, what Kit violated, what departure changes, when Helen's reversal registers, where attention drops and whether the hand works without explanation.

## Author's response and choices

The author asked for a thorough rework towards a less formulaic, more inspired and roughly "15%" more accessible and producible play. The percentage was treated as a direction, not a runtime or word-count quota.

During planning the author chose to retain the slow reveal and the main dramatic turns while allowing scene divisions and dialogue to be rebuilt. They accepted replacing a required onstage bodily transformation with legible shared activity and familiar company. A subsequent clarification expressly retained Helen as her own self-representation inside her mind, her idea of herself in these contexts.
