## 1. Assemble a modest recording

Use three distinct character performers and the narration arrangement proposed in the production notes. A separate narrator is preferable for the first test. If narration is doubled, record who doubles and test whether listeners confuse narration with that character's knowledge. Sal and Neri have no speaking roles.

One quiet room, a recorder and ordinary handling sounds are sufficient. Use only the sparse sound needed by this draft; a polished mix is unnecessary. One facilitator can operate the recorder and keep a cue log. Actors may hold scripts; listeners must not watch the performance. A recording lets everyone hear the same take without seeing an actor gesture that repairs an inaudible action.

Before recording:

1. Freeze the script version and date recorded in `PREAMBLE.md`, including its document layout revision. Record the Git commit containing it, plus the script's exact SHA-256. From the repository root, `shasum -a 256 stories/the-spare-room/adaptations/audio/SCRIPT.md` gives the file checksum. If uncommitted, say so, record the base commit and checksum, and retain an exact copy with the session files. Preserve the accompanying preamble, conventions and production notes too, so the recorded performance's instructions remain identifiable. Do not describe the base commit alone as the performed revision.
2. Rehearse the bag, chair, sleeve, invitations and final hand once. Perform only the labelled narration and dialogue. Never read technical directions aloud to repair missing information; record any necessary text change before freezing the final recording script.
3. Check level and intelligibility on an ordinary single speaker at conversational volume. Essential actions must survive mono playback. Check headphones too if used, but no fact may depend on left/right position.
4. Confirm that harbour sound belongs to Helen's experience and is not a move to another physical location. Kit's access can close while Helen's experience continues. Ruth's later invitation is separate and ends promptly. Do not add digital access noises or a farewell effect implying death.

Record a complete, naturally performed read, including intended narration, sounds, silences and any proposed credits. Allow pleasure, embarrassment and refusal their time. Do not ask actors to meet a words-per-minute rate, elongate pauses to reach 45 minutes, or hurry a long read. Log restarts and technical delays separately from dramatic pauses.

Keep the raw take, editing files, exact script copy and listening export under repository-root `dist/stories/the-spare-room/adaptations/audio/en/`, using a dated session subdirectory and descriptive filenames. Preserve the raw take. Editing may remove technical interruptions and use logged pickups; do not silently shorten acted pauses. Record the actual listening-export duration, the uninterrupted performance duration if separately measurable, technical interruptions, edits and credit duration. A synthetic scratch recording must be labelled synthetic and cannot establish human performance timing or emotional effectiveness.

## 2. Listen without a briefing

Invite a small group, ideally four to six people unfamiliar with the script, including some who do not normally seek science fiction. This is a practical development sample, not a representative survey. Record prior familiarity, usual listening habits and playback conditions using listener IDs.

Give only the title, listening arrangements and a request for candid reactions. Provide no synopsis, visible manuscript, room rules, premise explanation or list of protected events. Do not circulate the questions below before playback. The facilitator's checklist in section 4 is not audience material.

Play the complete recording without commentary or planned breaks. Provide an elapsed-time display if convenient. Listeners may jot a time or memorable phrase when something catches or loses them, but note-taking is optional. The facilitator logs conspicuous reactions with timecodes, without treating silence as boredom or laughter as understanding. If playback fails, log where and for how long; do not count that interruption as a dramatic attention loss.

After playback, allow five to ten quiet minutes for each listener's written or individually recorded response. Collect these before any group discussion, corrections or replay. Oral responses should be taken separately so a confident explanation cannot become everyone's first impression. Retain uncertainty and conflicting accounts.

## 3. Individual response sheet

Before handing out these questions, ask each listener privately: “Tell me what happened in your own words, including anything you couldn't make sense of.” Preserve that unaided account. Then start with experience, before testing comprehension. Ask for a line, action or approximate time when possible; a listener need not remember exact wording. The questions naming specific events below test prompted comprehension; keep those answers distinct from spontaneous recall and do not correct either account.

1. Where did you want to keep listening? Where did your attention wander, and what was happening then?
2. Which moment moved, amused or unsettled you? Which moment has stayed with you?
3. Whose company did you enjoy? Was there anyone you wanted more or less time with?
4. Would you recommend the piece to someone? Who, and why or why not?
5. Where does Ruth live? When did you become reasonably certain, and what helped you decide?
6. How did the quiet trial go? Why did Ruth then want to move, and why did Helen stop her?
7. What did Kit receive from Helen? How did they receive it, and what did they do with it? What could each person still experience afterwards?
8. What happened with the chair, the bag and the coat sleeve? Who did what?
9. What happened when Ruth tried Helen's experience herself? What changes when Ruth finally leaves? What remains possible between them?
10. What happened at the final handholding? Who offered, who accepted and who began to leave? What could Helen have chosen instead?
11. Where were you productively curious, and where were you lost? Did any passage feel repetitive, or clear but uninteresting?

After collecting individual answers, discuss a few specific disagreements and attention losses. Ask “What did you hear that gave you that impression?” before explaining anything. Replay a passage only now, and label any changed response as prompted or second-hearing evidence. Ask performers separately where they needed private explanation, where action or narration was awkward, and where the family became enjoyable or ceased to feel alive.

## 4. Interpret findings and revise

For the facilitator, check whether listeners can follow these basic events and available choices:

- The first move stalls because agreement was overstated. The quiet trial succeeds; Ruth chooses the life at Sal's, rather than being expelled for failing it.
- The room is within Helen; Ruth is alive and autonomous. Earlier ordinary exits do not end her residence. The move ends her automatic ability to summon Helen while allowing later visits.
- Helen's fear of Ruth becoming unfamiliar turns her into the obstruction. The remembered chair and Helen's withholding of an ordinary bag affect the action; Ruth is not transported inside the bag.
- Kit receives a fragment of Helen's present recollection through the still-open invitation, then uses it against her. They do not search her past. Helen closes their access, which remains closed at the ending. Helen retains her harbour.
- Ruth's separate invitation ends when she asks. She refuses Helen's help with the sleeve and accepts Kit's; that injury is not immediately repaired.
- Kit is free to leave. Helen recalls them, accepts the nearly withdrawn hand and initiates their departure. Choosing company does not restore Kit's access.

Agreement on these events matters; agreement about motives, generosity or future consequences does not. Listeners need not explain the civilisation or produce a moral lesson. Record which listener understood or missed which event instead of hiding differences in a single score.

Place each finding against a timecode **and** the script's A1–A7 section ID plus a cue or short quotation. Use A1 for the stalled move and calls; A2 for the trial and choice to move; A3 for the cake, chair and bag; A4 for Kit's received fragment and exclusion; A5 for Helen's fear and Ruth's separate invitation; A6 for departure and the sleeve; A7 for the missed meal and final choice. These identifiers are facilitator tools, not an audience briefing. Separate what was heard or observed from the team's proposed explanation:

| Finding | Likely revision to investigate |
| --- | --- |
| Confusing action: listeners cannot tell who acts, receives an invitation or retains access. | Clarify the decisive dialogue, sparse narration or sound perspective. Check audibility before adding exposition. |
| Repetitive action: listeners understand the exchange but hear no new development. | Remove a repeated negotiation or combine beats; preserve the consequential turn. Do not solve it by faster delivery. |
| Insufficient emotional investment: listeners follow events but do not care to continue. | Examine desire, humour, specificity and time to react. Test whether Sal's welcome, the burnt-crust anticipation, Helen's fear and Ruth's attempted sharing are actually felt. A clearer premise alone will not solve this. |

A passage may have more than one problem. One listener's response is worth recording; do not impose a numerical pass threshold on this small group. Pay particular attention to repeated losses in the opening and harbour, while checking what pleasure would disappear with a cut. Distinguish an attentive silence from a reported lapse.

Compare measured duration with the 45-minute target and recommend keeping, cutting or developing identified passages. State the deviation plainly; do not infer runtime from word count once performed timing exists. If the natural read is substantially short, investigate what emotional movement needs development before adding material. If long, identify repetition before compressing earned reactions.

Revise the script and relevant companion documents, updating the version in the preamble and recording the findings each change addresses and the questions left open in the production notes. Time a further performed read when material changes affect duration. Test revised passages with fresh unbriefed listeners where possible; returning listeners can assess change but cannot validate first-hearing clarity. A materially changed full draft needs a complete further read, not a sum of isolated estimates.
