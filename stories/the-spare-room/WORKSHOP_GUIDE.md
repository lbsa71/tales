# Workshop Guide — *The Spare Room*

Second-draft checkpoint, 22 September 2026. This is a plan for a modest, fully blocked, script-in-hand workshop. No workshop, audience response or measured performance duration is recorded yet. A completed rewrite does not establish readiness for production.

## Purpose and preparation

Test whether three actors can make the decisive actions legible with ordinary means, and whether the evening offers enough pleasure and changing activity to sustain attention. Use the [current chapter manuscript](chapters/README.md) and its generated English reading copy. PDF publication is handled by the repository's CI; do not treat an older packet PDF as the current script.

Use three performers, one playing space and one entrance. Assemble the ordinary table, chairs, swimming bag, coat, scarf, flask, mugs and spectacles listed in the manuscript. Neri and Sal are neither seen nor heard. No projections, recorded voices, scenic changes or effects are needed.

Give the performers and director the [room rules](CONCEPTUAL_MAP.md). Block the whole piece before the audience session, including the packing, chair moves, offstage calls and sleeve. Use a single, mutually agreed sightline for the harbour. The simple dance turn need not look accomplished. Helen must remain audibly and visibly responsive while attending to the harbour.

The onstage Helen is her contextual self-representation within her mind. This is private actor understanding, not a spectator briefing or a requirement to mime another body. Essential consequences must be carried by what people say and do.

Do not read explanatory stage directions aloud. Do not give spectators a synopsis that reveals the mind-room, the access distinction or the ending. A title, cast and ordinary practical information are sufficient.

## Before the audience enters

Run the decisive sequences once in order, with a script holder checking continuity rather than supplying interpretation:

1. Ruth cannot call Kit back; she can reach Helen beyond the same entrance. Keep both attempts distinct and audible.
2. Kit requests and receives an invitation. Cold, common sightline and the unspoken shoe establish a shared experience that Ruth lacks. Helen answers Ruth without first having to be brought out of a trance.
3. Ruth keeps the quiet promise. Helen finishes voluntarily. The direct statement about Ruth and the room being within Helen arrives before Ruth chooses Sal.
4. Ruth packs and moves the remembered chair. Helen takes the bag; both hold a handle. The prop is not treated as a means of transport for Ruth's person.
5. Kit exploits a fragment against Helen's obstruction. Helen explicitly closes the view; Kit loses the water. The offstage calling contrast repeats, proving Ruth's access continues.
6. Helen takes the bag to the entrance before reassurance. Ruth asks for a look at the harbour, receives a separate invitation, asks to stop and is released immediately. Kit is absent and gains no access from this invitation.
7. Ruth's final exit follows a brief agreement about moving and future contact. The sleeve refusal and acceptance can happen without a spoken explanation.
8. Helen returns to the established harbour activity. Kit's view is still closed. Kit starts leaving, Helen recalls them, and Helen eventually sets the activity aside to take the offered hand. Kit does not pull her into agreement.

Track the actual positions of bag, coat, spectacles and all three chairs across movement breaks. Resolve accidental omissions in blocking; record ambiguities in the text for revision. Do not invent a lighting code or extra line that secretly supplies a missing essential fact.

## Audience session

Invite a small group who have not read the play, including people who do not seek out science fiction. Let them watch continuously. An observer notes elapsed time, clear laughter and visible attention shifts against a line or action. Do not assume silence is boredom or laughter is comprehension.

Time the performance from the opening action to the final exit. Record any interruption caused by reading or handling scripts. Do not stretch silences or add business to achieve seventy minutes. Distinguish spoken duration, played action and workshop interruptions when reporting the result.

Collect brief individual accounts before discussion so that one confident explanation does not overwrite other experiences. Do not first tell the group what the play was supposed to mean.

Ask:

- Where does Ruth live? At what moment did you become reasonably certain?
- What did Kit receive from Helen, and what did Kit choose to do with it?
- When did Helen become the person stopping the move? What made that change apparent?
- What changes for Ruth and Helen when Ruth leaves? Could they meet again?
- Where did your attention drift? Were you confused, waiting for something new, or interested in something the play moved past?
- What happened at the final handholding? What alternatives seemed available to Helen?
- When did you enjoy being with each person, apart from understanding their argument?

During discussion, ask for moments and actions before interpretations. Preserve minority readings in the notes rather than merging them into a consensus. Afterwards ask the actors which transitions needed extra explanation, where maintaining the rules distracted from playing the scene, and which exchanges became repetitive aloud.

## Decisions after the workshop

Agreement about basic events and choices is the clarity test. Agreement about the generosity, fear or possible future cost of the final gesture is not required. The audience need not reconstruct the technology or all the private chronology.

If several spectators misunderstand the same decisive event, first revise its words or action. Do not make more sophisticated acting the default remedy. If a sequence is clear but repeatedly loses attention, cut repetition or change the dramatic activity before increasing delivery speed.

If spectators believe Ruth spoiled the quiet trial, preserve its success more clearly. If Kit seems to know everything, reduce or clarify the received fragment. If Ruth's departure resembles death, deletion or a magical ceremony, make the ordinary destination and loss of automatic contact clearer. If the final hand appears compelled, revisit Kit's departure attempt and Helen's recall before adding dialogue about choice.

Record findings in a dated workshop note: script version, participants' familiarity, production conditions, timing, observations tied to locations, individual comprehension accounts, performer difficulties and agreed revisions. Keep observed evidence distinct from proposed explanations. Add the checkpoint and resulting decisions to the development diary; schedule a further test only after the relevant revisions.
